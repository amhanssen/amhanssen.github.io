Case study · Editorial design · 2026

VOWS

A magazine about modern, nontraditional weddings for Gen Z and women in their 20s and 30s, printed and bound by hand, then rebuilt as an interactive website.

My role

Concept, writing and editing, editorial design, typography, print production and web design

Timeline

10 weeks · Spring 2026

Course

Typography III & Information Architecture, with Katherine Hughes & Rita Ferreira

Tools

InDesign, Illustrator, Photoshop, Figma

The idea

Bridal magazines sell one idea of a perfect wedding. VOWS shows the rest.

Traditional bridal magazines are built on an idealized, luxury look. I wanted a magazine for weddings that look like the people in them: courthouse ceremonies, small budgets, rainy days and couples who do it their own way.

The audience is Gen Z and women in their 20s and 30s who follow fashion, art and social media. Five keywords guided every choice: nontraditional, editorial, bold, playful and expressive.

Building the system

Weeks 1–2

Before any spreads, I organized the content into sections and built a flexible grid. Type galleys tested how each typeface pairing handled headlines, body text and captions.

A type galley, comparing typefaces for headings and body text.

Testing hierarchy through size, weight and contrast.

Experimental spreads

Week 3

I pushed each layout in a different direction, changing image scale, type emphasis and pacing, to see how far the grid could stretch while still feeling like one magazine.

“Could I afford to get married?”: big type over a full-bleed photo.

“Are you drowning in love?”: an underwater photo carrying the headline.

“Lucky days to get married”: dates as an illustrated list.

Smaller, more personal

Week 3 critique

In critique I realized my 10 × 13 in format felt too big and too formal. A wedding magazine should feel intimate, so I resized everything to 8 × 9.5 in, and then rethought the color and ornament to match.

Before

Week 3: “Rainy Days Aren’t Bad Luck,” black and white at 10 × 13 in.

After

Printed: the same idea in wine on butter yellow at 8 × 9.5 in, paired with “Wedding Superstitions.”

  • Resized from 10 × 13 to 8 × 9.5 in, so it feels like a book you hold, not a poster.
  • Replaced black with deep wine on butter yellow and cream, for a romantic tone without the luxury clichés.
  • Turned the flowers and lace into a recurring motif across the magazine and, later, the website.

Reworking a feature

Weeks 3–5

Before

First draft: a single photo with the headline set over it.

After

Printed: the photo bleeds across the spread, and the gold headline floats in the water.

  • Let the image cross the gutter, so the spread feels like one continuous scene.
  • Swapped the bold sans headline for a gold serif with a decorative initial, which feels more like the story.

Print to screen

Weeks 6–10

The second half of the project turned the magazine into a seven-page website. I mapped a sitemap, made black-and-white wireframes, then rebuilt each section for scrolling: bigger type, narrower columns, full-bleed images and oversized pull quotes.

Reflection

“The project started to come together as a complete system rather than a series of individual layouts.”

What worked

Resizing the magazine. A smaller page made every story feel more personal, and it changed how I designed the rest.

What I learned

Print and screen need different pacing. On the website I had to slow readers down with scale, white space and big quotes.

Next time

I’d plan the website alongside the magazine, so ideas like the scrolling feature could shape the print version too.
